# Magnets and Magnetic Fields — unit plan (OLEVEL_PHYS / C09_1)

Chapter 9, spreads **9.1 Magnets** (pages 198–199) and **9.2 Magnetic fields** (pages
200–201). A **phenomena** unit: the student observes, names, sorts and draws. English-only
(`bilingual: false`), so no `vn*` twins. First unit of the track — nothing to recap.

Stops before 9.3 (a current's magnetic field): "making a magnet with a coil" is mentioned
on slide 9 as the strongest method and left for `C09_2`.

## Objective

The student can name the **poles** of a magnet and explain *north-seeking*; state the
force law (**like poles repel, unlike poles attract; the closer, the greater the force**);
explain **induced magnetism** and predict the induced pole (always the *opposite* of the
pole nearest it); distinguish a **permanent** magnet (steel) from a **temporary** one
(iron); name the **magnetic** (ferromagnetic) metals **iron, nickel, cobalt** and steel,
the **non-magnetic** metals brass, copper, zinc, tin, aluminium, and sort **hard**
(steel, Alcomax, Magnadur) from **soft** (iron, Mumetal) magnetic materials with their uses;
know **ferrous / non-ferrous**; explain magnetism with **atomic magnets** and why hammering
or heating **demagnetises**; describe a **magnetic field** and plot a **field line** with a
**plotting compass**; state that field lines run N → S and that the direction is the force
on an N pole; read field strength from line spacing; find a **neutral point**; explain
**magnetic screening**; describe the **Earth's field** (a weak bar magnet with its magnetic
S pole near geographic north; **magnetic north** 1200 km from the North Pole; a compass is
useless at the poles because the lines are vertical).

## The mistakes the unit is built around

| Mistake | Where it is caught |
|---|---|
| "The N pole is attracted to the north, so the Earth's north is an N pole" | deck slide 22 (c14), Questions sq5, quiz 10 |
| Induced pole is the *same* as the pole next to it | deck a2, slide 7 (c4), Source Analysis 1, quiz 4 |
| Attraction proves something is a magnet (only **repulsion** does) | deck slide 24 (c16), Questions sq3, quiz 2 |
| All metals are magnetic / aluminium or copper "sticks" | deck a4, quiz 3 |
| Hard and soft swapped (steel for a core, iron for a permanent magnet) | deck a3, slide 11 (c6), quiz 5 |
| Field lines drawn S → N, or without arrows | deck slide 18 (c12), Source Analysis 2, quiz 6 |
| "The field is strongest where the lines are far apart" | deck slide 16 (c10), quiz 7 |
| Neutral point placed next to a pole, or thought to be "where the field is strongest" | deck a7, Source Analysis 1, quiz 8 |

## The deck (24 slides)

Slide 1 is the only unscored slide. `check` ids c1–c16, `activity` ids a1–a7.

1. **hero** — opener. Starter card: "A compass needle always swings round to point north.
   Nobody is turning it. In one sentence — what do you think is pulling it?"
2. **split** `POLES_FILINGS` — Magnetic poles. Dip a bar magnet in iron filings: they
   cling to the two ends, hardly at all to the middle. The force seems to come from two
   points: the **poles**. Note (write): *Pole: one of the two places on a magnet where the
   magnetic force is strongest.* **c1** — where do the filings cling? (ends / middle /
   evenly along it) → ends.
3. **split** `SUSPENDED_MAGNET` — Naming the poles. Hang a bar magnet from a thread: the
   Earth's field turns it until it lies roughly north–south. The end that points north is
   the **north-seeking pole (N pole)**; the other is the **south-seeking pole (S pole)**.
   Note (write): both definitions. **c2** — "north-seeking" means… (the pole that points
   towards north when the magnet can turn freely / the pole made at the North Pole / the
   pole that is attracted to iron) → points towards north.
4. **statement**, label Predict — **a1 predict**: the N poles of two bar magnets are
   brought close together. (They attract / They repel / Nothing happens until they touch).
   correct: repel. explain: *like poles repel; the next slide has the whole law.*
5. **split** `POLES_FORCES` — The force law. N near N: pushed apart; N near S: pulled
   together. The closer the poles, the greater the force. Note (write): **Like poles repel;
   unlike poles attract. The closer the poles, the greater the force.** **c3** — a magnet's
   S pole is brought towards another magnet's N pole, then moved closer still. (The pull
   gets stronger / The pull gets weaker / They begin to repel) → stronger.
6. **statement**, Predict — **a2 predict**: an unmagnetised iron nail hangs point-down just
   below the **N pole** of a magnet. Which pole appears at the *top* of the nail, nearest
   the magnet? (An N pole / An S pole / No pole — iron is not a magnet). correct: S pole.
   explain: *the magnet induces magnetism in the nail, and the induced pole nearest it is
   always the opposite one — that is why the nail is pulled up.*
7. **split** `INDUCED_POLES` — Induced magnetism. Iron and steel are attracted because the
   magnet **induces** magnetism in them; the induced pole nearest the magnet is the
   opposite of the magnet's pole, and unlike poles attract. Note (write): *Induced
   magnetism: magnetism a material gains because a magnet is near it. The induced pole
   nearest the magnet is the opposite pole.* **c4** — why does a steel paperclip stick to
   the S pole? (An N pole is induced in the paperclip next to the magnet / The paperclip
   was already a magnet / Steel is heavier than air) → N induced.
8. **compare** — Steel and iron after the magnet is taken away. Left column (steel, accent
   S-blue): keeps some of its induced magnetism → **permanent magnet**. Right column (iron):
   loses virtually all of it → **temporary magnet**. **a3 sort** — bins *Steel (hard)* /
   *Iron (soft)*; cards: "keeps its magnetism when the magnet is removed" (steel), "loses
   nearly all its magnetism at once" (iron), "difficult to magnetise" (steel), "easy to
   magnetise" (iron), "used to make permanent magnets" (steel), "used for the core of an
   electromagnet" (iron). 6 cards.
9. **steps** `STROKING` — Making a magnet, three ways. 1 Leave steel near a magnet: it
   becomes a weak permanent magnet. 2 Stroke it with one end of a magnet, always the same
   way, lifting the magnet well away on the return. 3 Put it in a long coil carrying a
   large direct current — the most effective method (next unit). **c5** — which method
   makes the strongest magnet? (Stroking / A coil with a large direct current / Leaving it
   near a magnet overnight) → coil.
10. **split** `MAGNETIC_METALS` — Magnetic and non-magnetic materials. A **magnetic
    material** can be magnetised and is attracted to a magnet. All strongly magnetic
    materials contain **iron, nickel or cobalt** (steel is mainly iron); these metals are
    **ferromagnetic**. **Non-magnetic**: brass, copper, zinc, tin, aluminium — and all
    non-metals. Note (write): *Magnetic metals: iron, nickel, cobalt (and steel). Non-
    magnetic metals: copper, brass, aluminium, zinc, tin.* **a4 sort** — bins *Magnetic* /
    *Non-magnetic*; cards: iron, nickel, cobalt, steel (magnetic); copper, brass,
    aluminium, zinc, tin (non-magnetic). 9 cards.
11. **compare** — Hard and soft magnetic materials. Hard (steel, Alcomax, Magnadur): hard
    to magnetise, keep their magnetism → permanent magnets. Soft (iron, Mumetal): easy to
    magnetise, magnetism temporary → cores of electromagnets and transformers, because the
    effect can be switched on, off or reversed. **c6** — why is iron, not steel, used for an
    electromagnet's core? (Iron loses its magnetism as soon as the current stops / Iron is
    cheaper / Iron makes a stronger permanent magnet) → loses magnetism.
12. **callout** `Languages` — Ferrous and non-ferrous. *Ferrum* is Latin for iron: iron and
    alloys containing iron (steel) are **ferrous**; aluminium, copper and the other
    non-magnetic metals are **non-ferrous**. **c7** — which is a ferrous metal? (Steel /
    Brass / Aluminium) → steel.
13. **split** `ATOMIC_MAGNETS` — Where magnetism comes from. Each electron acts as a tiny
    magnet; in some atoms the effects do not cancel, so each atom is a tiny magnet.
    Unmagnetised: atomic magnets point every way. Magnetised: more and more line up, and
    billions act as one big magnet. **c8** — what happens inside a steel bar as it is
    magnetised? (Its atomic magnets line up / New atoms are added / Electrons stop moving)
    → line up.
14. **split** `Hammer` icon, no diagram — Demagnetising. Hammering throws the atomic
    magnets out of line; heating to a high temperature does the same. (A third way, an
    alternating current in a coil, is in the next unit.) Note (write): *Demagnetise: to
    make a magnet lose its magnetism — by hammering or strong heating.* **c9** — a magnet
    dropped on the floor many times gets weaker. Why? (The knocks throw its atomic magnets
    out of line / It loses electrons / Its poles swap over) → out of line.
15. **statement**, Predict — **a5 predict**: iron filings are sprinkled on paper over a bar
    magnet and the paper is tapped. What pattern? (Curved lines running from one pole to
    the other / A ring round the middle of the magnet / Spread evenly everywhere).
    correct: curved lines.
16. **split** `FIELD_LINES_BAR` — The magnetic field. The filings become tiny magnets and
    are pulled into position: there is a **magnetic field** around the magnet. Field lines
    run from the **N pole to the S pole**; the field is **strongest where the lines are
    closest** — at the poles. Note (write): *Magnetic field: the region around a magnet
    where a magnetic material feels a force.* **c10** — where is the field strongest? (Where
    the lines are closest together, near the poles / Where the lines are furthest apart /
    Halfway along the side of the magnet) → closest.
17. **steps** `PLOTTING_COMPASS` — Plotting a field line. 1 Put a plotting compass near
    one pole; mark a dot at each end of the needle. 2 Move the compass so the back of the
    needle sits on the front dot; mark the new front. 3 Repeat to the other pole, join the
    dots: a **field line**. 4 Start somewhere else for the next line. **c11** — what does
    the compass needle's N end show? (The direction of the field at that point / The
    position of the magnet's N pole / Where the field is weakest) → direction.
18. **split** `COMPASS_DIRECTION` — Which way is the arrow? The direction of a field line
    is **the direction of the force on an N pole** — the way the N end of a compass points.
    So outside the magnet the arrows run N → S. Note (write): that definition. **c12** — a
    compass is placed beside the magnet, level with its middle. Which way does its N end
    point? (Towards the S pole end, along the field line / Towards the N pole end / Straight
    at the middle of the magnet) → towards the S pole end. (Check against `FIELD_LINES_BAR`:
    at the side of a bar magnet the lines run from the N end round to the S end.)
19. **statement**, Predict — **a6 predict**: two bar magnets are placed in a line, N pole
    facing N pole, a few centimetres apart. What happens to the field between them? (At one
    point between them the two fields cancel / The field there is twice as strong / The
    field is unchanged). correct: cancel.
20. **split** `TWO_MAGNETS` (two panels) — Fields combine. Unlike poles facing: the field
    between them is almost **uniform** (even strength, straight parallel lines). Like poles
    facing: the lines turn away from each other and there is a **neutral point** X, where
    the two fields exactly cancel and a magnetic material feels no force. Note (write):
    *Neutral point: a point where two magnetic fields cancel, so the magnetic force there
    is zero.* **a7 hotspot** on `TWO_MAGNETS` — "Tap the neutral point." correct target: X
    midway between the two N poles (like-poles panel); decoys: the gap between the unlike
    poles, the far outer end of one magnet, a point beside the like-poles panel's magnet.
21. **callout** `ShieldCheck` — Magnetic screening. Sensitive electronics are upset by
    fields from motors, transformers and the Earth. Enclose them in a layer of **soft**
    magnetic material (iron or nickel): it redirects the field so the lines pass through
    the case, not the equipment. **c13** — best material to screen a sensitive instrument?
    (A box of soft iron / A box of aluminium / A box of hard steel) → soft iron.
22. **split** `EARTH_FIELD` — The Earth's magnetic field. The Earth behaves as if it had a
    large, very weak bar magnet inside, probably from currents in its core. A compass's N
    end points north — and an N pole is attracted to an **S** pole — so the Earth's
    **magnetic S pole is in the north**, under a point in Canada called **magnetic north**.
    **c14** — why does the N end of a compass point north? (The Earth's magnetic S pole is
    near the geographic north, and unlike poles attract / There is a magnetic N pole at the
    North Pole / The Earth spins that way) → magnetic S pole.
23. **split** `Globe`, no diagram or reuse `EARTH_FIELD` with `ratio: 55` — Magnetic north
    is not the North Pole. It is over 1200 km away, because the Earth's magnetic axis is
    tilted from its axis of rotation. Near the poles the field lines are almost vertical,
    so a compass needle, which can only turn horizontally, is useless. **c15** — why is a
    compass no use near the North Pole? (The field lines there are nearly vertical / There
    is no magnetic field at the poles / It is too cold for the needle to turn) → vertical.
24. **stack** `variant: 'checklist'`, 3 columns — Can you do all six? items: define the N
    pole · state the force law · predict an induced pole · name three magnetic and three
    non-magnetic metals · plot a field line and arrow it · explain the Earth's field.
    **c16** — a metal bar is held near one pole of a magnet and is *repelled*. What do you
    know? (The bar must itself be a magnet — only a magnet can be repelled / The bar is iron
    / The bar is non-magnetic) → must be a magnet.

## Questions (5, three marks each; fresh situations)

- **sq1** A student hangs a bar magnet from a thread so it can turn freely. Explain how
  this shows which end is the N pole, and what the name "north-seeking pole" means.
  (1 the magnet turns and comes to rest lying north–south, pulled by the Earth's field;
  1 the end pointing north is the N pole; 1 "north-seeking" = the pole that seeks / points
  towards the north.) suggestedWords: `[['thread', 'freely'], ['Earth'], ['comes to rest']]`.
- **sq2** An iron nail and a steel nail are each touched against the S pole of a bar magnet
  and both stick. They are then pulled off. Explain why both stuck, and what is different
  about the two nails afterwards. (1 the magnet induces magnetism in each nail, with an N
  pole at the end nearest the S pole, and unlike poles attract; 1 the steel nail keeps some
  magnetism — a permanent magnet; 1 the iron nail loses almost all of it — temporary.)
  suggestedWords: `[['induced'], ['unlike'], ['hard', 'soft']]`.
- **sq3** Three identical-looking rods are: a brass rod, an iron rod, and a steel magnet.
  Using only one known bar magnet, describe how to identify each rod. (1 brass: no force
  from either pole — non-magnetic; 1 iron: attracted to both poles at either end, never
  repelled — induced magnetism; 1 the magnet: one of its ends is repelled by one pole of the
  known magnet — repulsion is the only test for a magnet.) suggestedWords:
  `[['pole'], ['test'], ['either end']]`.
- **sq4** Describe how to use a plotting compass to draw one magnetic field line around a
  bar magnet, and state how the finished drawing shows where the field is strongest. (1 put
  the compass near one pole, mark dots at the two ends of the needle, move it so the back of
  the needle is on the front dot, and repeat; 1 join the dots and arrow the line in the
  direction the N end pointed, from N to S; 1 the field is strongest where lines are closest
  together — at the poles.) suggestedWords: `[['needle'], ['dots'], ['arrow']]`.
- **sq5** Explain why the N end of a compass needle points towards the Earth's north, and
  why a compass is of no use close to the North Pole. (1 the Earth behaves like a bar magnet
  with a magnetic **S** pole near the geographic north; 1 unlike poles attract, so the needle's
  N end is pulled towards it; 1 near the pole the field lines are nearly vertical, so there
  is almost no horizontal pull to turn a needle that can only swing horizontally.)
  suggestedWords: `[['bar magnet'], ['unlike'], ['vertical']]`.

## Source Analysis (3)

1. **MCQ** on `INDUCED_POLES`: "The magnet's N pole is at the bottom. Which poles are
   induced in the iron bar?" A S at the top, N at the bottom (correct) · B N at the top, S
   at the bottom · C N at both ends · D none, because iron is not a magnet.
2. **MCQ** on `FIELD_LINES_BAR`: "The compass on the field line has its red end pointing
   along the arrow. What does that tell you about the arrow?" A it points from the N pole
   towards the S pole, the direction of the force on an N pole (correct) · B it points to
   the nearest pole · C it points to where the field is weakest · D it shows the way the
   magnet is moving.
3. **Written** on `TWO_MAGNETS`: "Describe the field between the two magnets in each panel,
   and explain what would happen to a small piece of iron placed exactly at X." (1 unlike
   poles: straight, evenly spaced lines — an almost uniform field; 1 like poles: the lines
   bend away from each other and X is a neutral point where the two fields cancel; 1 the
   iron at X feels no magnetic force / is not pulled either way.) `imageAlt` must say
   which panel is which and where X sits.

## Quiz (10 MCQ, 12 minutes) — key A B C D spread 3 · 3 · 2 · 2

1. Where on a bar magnet is the magnetic force strongest? → the two ends (B)
2. One end of a metal rod is attracted by the N pole of a magnet. What is certain? → it is
   a magnetic material — it might be a magnet or just iron; only repulsion would prove it
   is a magnet (C) · distractors: it must be a magnet; it is non-magnetic; it must be steel
3. Which list has only non-magnetic metals? → copper, aluminium, brass (A)
4. The S pole of a magnet is held above a steel pin, which jumps up. Which pole was induced
   at the top of the pin? → N (D)
5. Which is the best material for a permanent magnet, and why? → steel, hard, keeps its
   magnetism (B)
6. Field lines around a magnet are drawn with arrows. The arrows point… → from N to S,
   the way the force on an N pole acts (A)
7. In a field diagram the lines are closest together at P and far apart at Q. → the field
   is stronger at P (C)
8. A neutral point is… → where two fields cancel so the force is zero (D)
9. A permanent magnet is heated strongly and loses its magnetism because… → its atomic
   magnets are thrown out of line (A)
10. The N end of a compass points north because… → the pole of the Earth's field near the
    geographic north is a magnetic S pole (B)
