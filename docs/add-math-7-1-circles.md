# Add Maths 7.1 — The equation of a circle

Source section: Cambridge IGCSE and O Level Additional Mathematics coursebook (3rd
edition), section 7.1, pages 157–165. Read in the Cambridge GO reader on 2026-09-30.

This file has three parts: the beats of the notes in our own words, a map of the
exercise's question types, and a fresh problem set in the same style with answers. None
of the problems below are the book's. Every answer was checked by script.

## Part 1 — The beats of the notes

The section runs in this order. Page numbers are the book's.

1. **What a circle is (p. 157).** The set of all points a fixed distance (the radius)
   from a fixed point (the centre). The word used is *locus*.
2. **Discover the pattern (p. 157).** A class discussion: draw seven circles with
   graphing software, record each centre and radius in a table, and work out how to read
   them straight off the equation. The point is for students to notice the sign flip and
   the square root themselves.
3. **Derive the equation (p. 158).** Take any point P(x, y) on a circle with centre
   C(a, b). Drop a right-angled triangle: across is x − a, up is y − b, the hypotenuse
   is r. Pythagoras gives (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r². The book calls this *completed
   square form*.
4. **Read it (worked example 1).** Write down centre and radius from the equation. One
   part has a radius that is a surd.
5. **Write it (worked example 2).** Build the equation from a centre and a radius,
   taking care when a coordinate is negative.
6. **Diameter given (worked example 3, p. 159).** The centre is the midpoint of the
   diameter; the radius is the distance from the centre to either end.
7. **Expanded general form (p. 159).** Multiply out to x² + y² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0, with
   centre (−g, −f) and radius √(g² + f² − c). Two tells that an equation is a circle:
   x² and y² have the same coefficient, and there is no xy term.
8. **Back again (worked example 4, p. 160).** Complete the square in x and in y to get
   centre and radius from the general form.
9. **Three right-angle facts (p. 160).** The angle in a semicircle is 90°. The
   perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it. A tangent is perpendicular to
   the radius at the point of contact. Each is then turned round: a right angle at B
   means AC is a diameter; the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the
   centre; a line at right angles to a radius at the circumference is a tangent.
10. **Two points are not enough (class discussion, p. 160).** "Two points fix a unique
    circle": always, sometimes or never? The centre can be anywhere on the perpendicular
    bisector of the two points.
11. **Two points and a radius (worked example 5, p. 161).** Find the perpendicular
    bisector, write the centre as (a, b) on it, use the radius to get a quadratic. Two
    answers, shown as two circles on one diagram.
12. **Three points are enough (class discussion, p. 162).** "Three non-collinear points
    fix a unique circle." The centre is where the perpendicular bisectors meet.
13. **Three points (worked example 6, pp. 162–163).** Perpendicular bisectors of two
    chords, solved simultaneously for the centre, then the radius. A closing discussion
    asks for the algebraic route instead: three simultaneous equations from the general
    form, then compare the two methods.

## Part 2 — What the exercise asks

Exercise 7.1 is on pages 163–165: 21 questions, the last two marked as challenge.

| Type | Book questions | What it asks | How it climbs |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Read centre and radius | 1 (ten parts) | Completed square form to centre and radius | Centred at the origin, then a surd radius, then a common factor to divide out, then one bracket, then two |
| B. Write the equation | 2 (four parts) | Centre and radius to equation | Origin, integers, a surd radius, a fractional centre |
| C. General form | 3 (six parts), 8 | Complete the square to find centre and radius | Coefficient 1 first, then a common factor of 4; question 8 is the "show that … hence" version |
| D. Centre and one point | 4, 10 | Radius from a distance; question 10 borrows the centre from another circle | One step, then two |
| E. Diameter | 5 | Midpoint and distance | Single question |
| F. Sketches and touching an axis | 6, 7, 13 | Sketch; a circle touching an axis; radius, a point and a tangent axis giving two answers | Direct, then a quadratic |
| G. Chords and bisectors | 9, 12, 15 | Show the centre lies on a line; two points and a radius; two points and a line for the centre | "Show that", then two-answer problems |
| H. Tangent at a point | 14 | Show a point is on the circle, then the tangent there | Single question |
| I. Right angles | 11, 16 | Show a side is a diameter or an angle is 90°, then the equation | Two questions |

| K. Designs | 18, 19 | Draw the Olympic rings; a four-circle design inside a larger circle | Graphing software |
| Challenge | 20, 21 | Three equal circles inside a larger one; show four points lie on a circle by four methods, then with Ptolemy's theorem | Ends with a reflection on which method is best |

## Part 3 — Fresh problems in the same style

### A. Read the centre and radius

Write down the centre and the radius. Give surd radii in simplest form.

- A1. x² + y² = 49
- A2. x² + y² = 20
- A3. 3x² + 3y² = 75
- A4. x² + (y + 6)² = 4
- A5. (x − 3)² + (y + 1)² = 64
- A6. (x + 5)² + (y − 9)² = 12
- A7. (x + ½)² + (y + 4)² = 50

### B. Write the equation

Find the equation of the circle.

- B1. Centre (0, 0), radius 9
- B2. Centre (−2, 7), radius 5
- B3. Centre (4, −3), radius 3√2
- B4. Centre (3/2, −1/4), radius 2
- B5. Centre (−6, 0), radius √7

### C. From general form

Find the centre and the radius.

- C1. x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 12 = 0
- C2. x² + y² + 10x − 2y + 10 = 0
- C3. x² + y² − 8y − 9 = 0
- C4. x² + y² + 2x + 6y − 8 = 0
- C5. 2x² + 2y² + 4x − 20y + 2 = 0
- C6. 4x² + 4y² − 4x + 24y + 1 = 0
- C7. Show that x² + y² − 4x + 12y = 9 can be written as (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r².
  Hence write down the centre and the radius.
- C8. Explain why x² + y² + 4x − 6y + 15 = 0 is not the equation of a circle.

### D. A centre and one point

- D1. Find the equation of the circle with centre (1, −2) that passes through (6, 10).
- D2. Find the equation of the circle with centre (−3, 4) that passes through (1, 1).
- D3. A circle passes through (7, 1) and has the same centre as
  x² + y² − 6x + 2y − 15 = 0. Find its equation.

### E. A diameter

Find the equation of the circle with diameter AB.

- E1. A(2, 3), B(10, 9)
- E2. A(−3, 5), B(5, 1)
- E3. A(−1, −8), B(11, −2)

### F. Sketches and touching an axis

- F1. Sketch the circle (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 9. Mark the centre, the point where it
  touches an axis, and the exact points where it crosses the other axis.
- F2. A circle has centre (−4, 5) and touches the x-axis. Find its equation.
- F3. A circle has centre (6, −2) and touches the y-axis. Find its equation.
- F4. A circle of radius 4 touches both axes and its centre is in the first quadrant.
  Find its equation. How many circles of radius 4 touch both axes?
- F5. A circle has radius 5 and passes through (3, 1). The x-axis is a tangent to the
  circle. Find the two possible equations.

### G. Chords and perpendicular bisectors

- G1. P(−2, 3) and Q(6, 7) lie on a circle. Show that the centre of the circle lies on
  the line y = −2x + 9.
- G2. A circle passes through A(1, 2) and B(7, 4). Its centre lies on the line
  x + y = 9. Find the equation of the circle.
- G3. A circle passes through (0, 1) and (6, 3) and has radius 2√5. Find the two
  possible equations.
- G4. A circle passes through (−3, 0) and (1, 4) and has radius √10. Find the two
  possible equations.

### H. Tangent at a point

- H1. Show that P(5, 3) lies on the circle (x − 2)² + (y + 1)² = 25. Find the equation
  of the tangent at P.
- H2. Show that (−3, 1) lies on the circle x² + y² − 2x − 8y − 8 = 0. Find the equation
  of the tangent at that point.
- H3. Find the equation of the tangent to (x + 1)² + (y − 2)² = 16 at the point (3, 2).
  (Look at the radius before you calculate anything.)

### I. Right angles

- I1. A circle passes through P(−2, −1), Q(−1, 6) and R(6, 5). Show that PR is a
  diameter and find the equation of the circle.
- I2. A(−2, −6), B(4, 6) and C(8, 4) form a triangle. Show that angle ABC is a right
  angle. Hence find the equation of the circle through A, B and C.

### J. Three points

Find the equation of the circle through the three points.

- J1. (0, 0), (6, 0), (0, 8)
- J2. (1, 7), (8, 6), (7, −1)
- J3. (−1, 2), (3, 4), (5, 0)
- J4. (2, 9), (8, 1), (−4, −3)
- J5. Repeat J3 a second way. Write the circle as x² + y² + px + qy + c = 0, substitute
  the three points to get three equations, and solve them. Which method did you prefer?

### K. Designs

- K1. A logo has five rings of radius 3. The top three have centres (−7, 0), (0, 0) and
  (7, 0). The bottom two have centres (−3.5, −3) and (3.5, −3). Write the five
  equations. Show that the top rings do not touch each other, and that each bottom ring
  overlaps the two rings above it. Draw the logo with graphing software.
- K2. Four circles of radius 2 have centres (2, 2), (−2, 2), (−2, −2) and (2, −2).
  1. Show that each circle touches its two neighbours.
  2. Find the radius of the smallest circle, centred at the origin, that contains all
     four.
  3. Find the radius of the largest circle, centred at the origin, that fits in the gap
     between them.

### Challenge-style

- X1. Three circles of radius 3 have centres (−3, 0), (3, 0) and (0, 3√3).
  1. Show that each circle touches the other two.
  2. Find the point that is the same distance from all three centres, and that distance.
  3. Find the equation of the smallest circle that contains all three.
  4. Find the radius of the small circle that fits in the gap in the middle and touches
     all three.
- X2. One circle of radius 1 is centred at the origin. Six more circles of radius 1 are
  arranged around it so that each touches the central circle and its two neighbours.
  One of the six has centre (2, 0). Find the other five centres, and the radius of the
  smallest circle that contains the whole design.
- X3. A(−2, 5), B(6, 1), C(4, −3) and D(−3, −2).
  1. Use the perpendicular bisectors of AB and BC to find the circle through A, B and
     C. Show that D lies on it.
  2. Show that angle ABC = 90° and angle ADC = 90°. Explain why this also proves that
     the four points lie on one circle, and say what AC is.
  3. Ptolemy's theorem says that for a quadrilateral ABCD with all four corners on a
     circle, AC × BD = AB × CD + BC × DA. Check that it holds here.
  4. Which method was quickest for these four points? Would it always be?
- X4. The equation x² + y² − 6x + 2y + k = 0 represents a circle.
  1. Find the range of values of k.
  2. Find k if the circle touches the x-axis.
  3. Find k if the circle touches the y-axis.
  4. Find k and the radius if the circle passes through the origin.
- X5. A circle touches both axes and passes through (2, 1). Find the two possible
  equations.

## Answers

**A.** A1 (0, 0), 7. A2 (0, 0), 2√5. A3 (0, 0), 5. A4 (0, −6), 2. A5 (3, −1), 8.
A6 (−5, 9), 2√3. A7 (−½, −4), 5√2.

**B.** B1 x² + y² = 81. B2 (x + 2)² + (y − 7)² = 25. B3 (x − 4)² + (y + 3)² = 18.
B4 (x − 3/2)² + (y + 1/4)² = 4. B5 (x + 6)² + y² = 7.

**C.** C1 (3, −2), 5. C2 (−5, 1), 4. C3 (0, 4), 5. C4 (−1, −3), 3√2.
C5 (−1, 5), 5 (divide by 2 first). C6 (½, −3), 3 (divide by 4 first).
C7 (x − 2)² + (y + 6)² = 49; centre (2, −6), radius 7.
C8 Completing the square gives (x + 2)² + (y − 3)² = −2. A sum of two squares cannot be
negative, so no point satisfies it.

**D.** D1 (x − 1)² + (y + 2)² = 169. D2 (x + 3)² + (y − 4)² = 25.
D3 Centre (3, −1); (x − 3)² + (y + 1)² = 20.

**E.** E1 (x − 6)² + (y − 6)² = 25. E2 (x − 1)² + (y − 3)² = 20.
E3 (x − 5)² + (y + 5)² = 45.

**F.** F1 Centre (3, −2), radius 3; touches the y-axis at (0, −2); crosses the x-axis at
(3 − √5, 0) and (3 + √5, 0). F2 (x + 4)² + (y − 5)² = 25. F3 (x − 6)² + (y + 2)² = 36.
F4 (x − 4)² + (y − 4)² = 16; four circles, one in each quadrant.
F5 The centre is (a, 5) because the point is above the axis; (3 − a)² + 16 = 25 gives
a = 0 or 6. x² + (y − 5)² = 25 and (x − 6)² + (y − 5)² = 25.

**G.** G1 Midpoint (2, 5); gradient of PQ is ½, so the bisector has gradient −2:
y − 5 = −2(x − 2), which is y = −2x + 9. The centre lies on the perpendicular bisector
of any chord. G2 Bisector y = −3x + 15 meets x + y = 9 at (3, 6);
(x − 3)² + (y − 6)² = 20. G3 (x − 4)² + (y + 1)² = 20 and (x − 2)² + (y − 5)² = 20.
G4 x² + (y − 1)² = 10 and (x + 2)² + (y − 3)² = 10.

**H.** H1 9 + 16 = 25, so P is on the circle. Radius gradient 4/3, tangent gradient
−3/4: 3x + 4y = 27. H2 Centre (1, 4), radius 5; 16 + 9 = 25. Radius gradient 3/4,
tangent gradient −4/3: 4x + 3y = −9. H3 The radius to (3, 2) is horizontal, so the
tangent is the vertical line x = 3.

**I.** I1 Gradient PQ = 7, gradient QR = −1/7, product −1, so angle PQR = 90° and PR is
a diameter. Centre (2, 2): (x − 2)² + (y − 2)² = 25. I2 Gradient AB = 2, gradient
BC = −½, product −1. AC is a diameter, centre (3, −1): (x − 3)² + (y + 1)² = 50.

**J.** J1 (x − 3)² + (y − 4)² = 25. J2 (x − 4)² + (y − 3)² = 25.
J3 (x − 2)² + (y − 1)² = 10. J4 (x − 1)² + (y − 2)² = 50.
J5 The equations are −p + 2q + c = −5, 3p + 4q + c = −25 and 5p + c = −25, giving
p = −4, q = −2, c = −5: x² + y² − 4x − 2y − 5 = 0, the same circle as J3.

**K.** K1 (x + 7)² + y² = 9, x² + y² = 9, (x − 7)² + y² = 9,
(x + 3.5)² + (y + 3)² = 9, (x − 3.5)² + (y + 3)² = 9. Top centres are 7 apart, more than
3 + 3, so they do not touch. A bottom centre is √(3.5² + 3²) = √21.25 ≈ 4.61 from each
centre above it, less than 6, so they overlap.
K2 Neighbouring centres are 4 apart, which equals 2 + 2. Each centre is 2√2 from the
origin, so the outer radius is 2√2 + 2 and the inner radius is 2√2 − 2.

**Challenge-style.**
X1 Each pair of centres is 6 apart, which equals 3 + 3. The point is (0, √3), at
distance 2√3 from each centre. Outer circle x² + (y − √3)² = (3 + 2√3)², radius
3 + 2√3. Gap circle radius 2√3 − 3.
X2 (1, √3), (−1, √3), (−2, 0), (−1, −√3), (1, −√3). Radius 3.
X3 The bisector of AB is x = 1; the bisector of BC is y − 3 = ½(x − 5); they meet at
(1, 1). Radius 5: (x − 1)² + (y − 1)² = 25, and D gives 16 + 9 = 25. Both right angles
stand on AC, so B and D each lie on the circle with AC as diameter. Ptolemy:
AC × BD = 10 × 3√10 = 30√10, and AB × CD + BC × DA = 20√10 + 10√10 = 30√10.
X4 Centre (3, −1), r² = 10 − k. k < 10; k = 9 (radius 1); k = 1 (radius 3); k = 0,
radius √10.
X5 The centre is (r, r): (2 − r)² + (1 − r)² = r² gives r = 1 or 5.
(x − 1)² + (y − 1)² = 1 and (x − 5)² + (y − 5)² = 25.
