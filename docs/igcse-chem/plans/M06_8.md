# 6.8 Transition Elements — unit plan (IGCSE_CHEM / M06_8)

Wolsey Hall Module 6, Topic Eight (6.8). Built from book spread **12.5 "The
transition elements"** (pages 154–155), read against **12.2 "Group I: the alkali
metals"** (the comparison the whole spread is built on) and **12.1** (where the
block sits), with the Chapter 12 Checkup (pages 158–159) as the guide to what the
exam asks. English-only (`bilingual: false`), so no `vn*` twins.

**The last topic of Module 6**, and the module ends in a multiple-choice quiz. So
the deck's last three slides, the Challenge tier of Practice and the last two quiz
items pull the three families together: alkali metals vs halogens vs transition
elements.

## Objective

The student can say where the transition elements are (the block between Group II
and Group III, from Period 4) and that they are all metals; name the ten to know;
give their physical properties against sodium — high density (iron over 8 times
sodium), high melting point (mercury the exception), hard and strong, good
conductors (silver best, copper next); give their chemical properties — much less
reactive than Group I, no clear trend, coloured compounds, catalysts (iron in making
ammonia); link each use to a property (steel, stainless steel with chromium,
titanium, pigments and glazes, copper wiring); make their salts from their oxides
and hydroxides. **Extended:** ions with different charges (Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺)
against Group I/II/III's fixed charges; more than one compound with another element
(Cu₂O/CuO, FeO/Fe₂O₃); the Roman numeral is the oxidation number; variable oxidation
numbers; name ⇄ formula both ways.

| Mistake | Where it is caught |

| Putting a Group I, II or III metal in the transition block | deck a1 · workbook f1 · quiz 1 |
| "How many times" found by subtracting, or dividing upside down | deck a3 · Source Analysis 1 |
| Forgetting mercury is the exception | deck c3 · workbook f3 |
| Calling a catalyst a reactant that gets used up | deck c7 · workbook f4 · quiz 4 · Questions sq3 |

| Reading the Roman numeral as a COUNT (of metal ions or of the other ion) | deck c10, c11 · workbook c1, c2 · Source Analysis 2 · Formulae hints |
| Reading a subscript (or the 3 in CO₃) as the oxidation number | deck c11 · quiz 7 |
| Group trends backwards (Group I vs Group VII) | deck a8 · workbook p2 · quiz 10 |

## The deck (21 slides)

1 opener (why is no bridge made of sodium?) · 2 recap of Group I (c1) · 3 where the
block is, `TABLE_BLOCK`, Draw This (c2) · 4 the ten to know, `TEN_TILES` (**sort
a1**: transition element or not) · 5 **predict a2**: two same-size blocks, how many
times heavier is the iron? · 6 high density, `DENSITY_BARS` (**estimate a3**:
7.9 ÷ 0.97 ≈ 8) · 7 high melting points and the mercury exception, `MELTING_BARS`
(c3) · 8 hard, strong, good conductors — silver, then copper (c4) · 9 much less
reactive than Group I, no clear trend — compare sodium with copper and nickel (c5) ·
10 coloured compounds, `COLOURED_COMPOUNDS` (c6) · 11 catalysts, iron in making
ammonia, `CATALYST_BED` (c7) · 12 two kinds of metal (**sort a4**: alkali metal or
transition element) · 13 each use comes from a property — steel, stainless steel,
titanium, pigments (**sort a5**: property → use) · 14 making their salts,
NiO + 2HCl (c8) · **Extended:** 15 ions with different charges, fixed vs variable
(c9) · 16 more than one compound with oxygen, `COPPER_IRON_OXIDES`, Draw This (c10)
· 17 the Roman numeral is the oxidation number, copper(I) chloride → CuCl (**sort
a6**: names by oxidation number) · 18 from a formula back to a name, FeBr₃ →
iron(III) bromide (c11) · **Module close:** 19 three families side by side,
`THREE_FAMILIES` (**sort a7**: seven described elements into alkali metal /
halogen / transition element / noble gas) · 20 which way reactivity goes (**hotspot
a8** on `TABLE_BLOCK`, labels stripped: tap the family whose reactivity decreases
down the group) · 21 countable recap (c12).

Activities by type: sort ×5, predict, estimate, hotspot. The `periodic` activity
only covers the first 20 elements, so the table recap is a hotspot on the unit's own
outline table (the names and legend are `class="lbl"` and stripped; the group
numerals are `class="keep"`). The book's worked examples (copper(I) chloride,
FeBr₃, the four oxides, the density table) are taught in the deck.

## Diagrams (authored, no third-party imagery)

`diagrams.js` holds eight: `TABLE_BLOCK` (outline table, block shaded, Groups I /
VII / VIII tinted, legend), `TEN_TILES` (the ten in their real places in the block),
`DENSITY_BARS` and `MELTING_BARS` (the book's data drawn to scale from a helper that

Colour: transition elements teal `#0e7490`, Group I amber `#b45309`, Group VII
purple `#7e22ce`, noble gases slate, oxide ion blue-violet `#4338ca` (as in 6.2).

## Formulae (`FORMULA_WRITE`, 10 items, in pairs)

copper(I) oxide Cu₂O · copper(II) oxide CuO · iron(II) oxide FeO · iron(III) oxide
Fe₂O₃ · iron(II) chloride FeCl₂ · iron(III) chloride FeCl₃ · copper(I) bromide CuBr
· copper(II) bromide CuBr₂ · iron(III) hydroxide Fe(OH)₃ · chromium(III) oxide
Cr₂O₃. Each pair is the same two elements with a different numeral. Every item is
neutral, in simplest numbers, and its numeral equals its cation charge (node check).
Hydroxide is written `OH`; the component brackets it as (OH)₃. Hints name the method,
never the charge being asked for.

## Practice (12)

melting point, reactivity (inline) · f3 silver / mercury / iron / chromium → fact

wrong · c3 identify element Q from QCl₂ and QCl₃.

## Questions (5, three marks each)

sq1 titanium vs potassium, three physical differences · sq2 tell a sodium compound
from an iron compound by colour · sq3 nickel as a catalyst, and why it is not
re-bought · sq4 name CrCl₂ and CrCl₃, and why chromium has two chlorides and
magnesium one · sq5 reactivity with water and the trend: alkali metals vs
transition elements.

## Source Analysis (3)

1 MCQ on `DENSITY_BARS`: how many times denser is nickel than sodium (≈ 9) · 2 MCQ
on `COPPER_IRON_OXIDES`: why iron(III) oxide is Fe₂O₃ · 3 written on `MELTING_BARS`:
the one metal that stays solid at 1500 °C, and why the other three fail.
