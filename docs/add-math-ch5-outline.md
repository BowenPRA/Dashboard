## Section by section

### Opener (pp. 99–100)

Four objectives: simple properties of log and exponential functions including ln x and
e^x; their graphs, including the transformed forms; the laws of logs with change of
base; and equations of the form a^x = b. The prerequisite check asks for a sketch of a
simple exponential, the inverse of an exponential drawn by reflection, and three
same-base index equations. Key words: logarithm, laws of logarithms, change of base
rule, natural logarithms.

### 5.1 Logarithms to base 10 (pp. 100–103)

- Motivation: an equation with the unknown in the power of 10, estimated first from a
  graph, then named as a logarithm. Notation lg / log and the calculator key.
- The rule linking the two forms, stated in symbols and in words, and the inverse-graph
  picture (reflection in y = x).
- A class discussion on why four basic facts are true.
- Worked examples: exponential form to log form and solve (two methods shown); log form
  to exponential form and solve; evaluating without a calculator by writing the number
  as a power of 10, including a surd.
- Exercise, five questions of six parts each: convert each way, solve each way to 3 s.f.,
  then evaluate without a calculator (whole powers, decimals, roots, mixed).

### 5.2 Logarithms to base a (pp. 104–106)

- Same ideas in a general base, the four facts again, and the conditions on the base and
  the argument.
- Worked examples: convert each way (two methods), and evaluate by writing the argument
  as a power of the base, including a fraction.
- Exercise, six questions: convert each way (numbers, then letters); solve simple log
  equations including unknown base and a linear argument; a long "find the value" set
  with surds and fractional answers; simplify logs of powers and roots of the base;
  two nested-log equations to finish.

### 5.3 The laws of logarithms (pp. 106–108)

- Multiplication, division and power laws with short proofs, plus the "log of a
  reciprocal" rule.
- Worked examples: combine logs into one; express in terms of x and y given two logs.
- Exercise, nine questions: write as a single log (including a whole number plus a log);
  single log then simplify; mixed simplifying; ratios of logs; "in terms of u", "in terms
  of x and y"; find values given two logs.
- Trap already in our notes: the worked example on the division law prints a ÷ between
  two logs where a − is meant. Still there in this edition. Never copy it.

### 5.4 Solving logarithmic equations (pp. 108–110)

- Key message: every root must be checked in the original equation.
- Worked example, two parts: a log equation that becomes a quadratic with one root
  rejected because a log of a negative is undefined; and an unknown-base equation where
  the negative base is rejected.
- Exercise, seven questions, climbing: logs on both sides; a constant on one side;
  equations leading to quadratics; unknown base; quadratics in a log (substitution);
  then two challenge questions on simultaneous equations mixing logs and indices.

### 5.5 Solving exponential equations (pp. 110–112)

- For equations that cannot be put to a common base: take logs of both sides.
- Worked examples: two "take logs" equations to 3 s.f. (the second with a linear
  exponent); one hidden quadratic solved by substitution, with the negative value
  rejected because a power is always positive.
- Exercise, eleven questions: a twelve-part drill from simple to different bases on each
  side; guided "show that, hence find" index-law questions; hidden quadratics, plain then
  with shifted exponents, then in disguised bases; three challenge questions (mixed
  bases, modulus equations, a modulus inequality in exact form).
- Ends with a reflection box about converting between the two forms.

### 5.6 Change of base (pp. 112–114)

- The rule, its proof, and the reciprocal special case. A note that some calculators
  have a general log key.
- Worked examples: change to base 10 and evaluate; solve an equation with two related
  bases, ending in a quadratic with one root rejected.
- Exercise, twelve questions: evaluate to 3 s.f.; "in terms of u" with base and argument
  swapped; express in another base; find one log from two others; a product of logs;
  mixed-base equations; guided substitution questions; reciprocal-type equations that
  become quadratics in a log; simultaneous equations, the last one a challenge.

### 5.7 Natural logarithms (pp. 114–115)

- e as an irrational number, ln as log to base e, the inverse-graph picture, and the
  statement that every earlier rule still applies. No worked examples.
- Exercise, fourteen questions: calculator evaluation of powers of e and of ln; exact
  values using the inverse relationship; solve to 3 s.f.; solve leaving answers in terms
  of ln; ln equations with linear arguments; exact-form questions including hidden
  quadratics in e^x; simultaneous equations; a factorising question; one challenge.

### 5.8 Practical applications (pp. 116–117)

- One worked example on a cooling model: value at the start, value at a given time, and
  the time to reach a given value.
- Exercise, six context questions: doubling growth, population decay, a draining
  container, a fish population halving, a house value doubling, and a growth model with
  two data points. The recurring asks are: initial value, value at a time, find the
  constant from a data point, and time to reach or pass a threshold.

### 5.9 Graphs of e^x and ln x (p. 118)

- Properties of each curve as bullet lists: axis intercept, where it exists, behaviour
  at each end, asymptote. They are mirror images in y = x.
- Exercise: two graphing-software investigations of what a constant k does to each
  family (inside the function, multiplying it, added to it), for positive and negative k.

### 5.10 Graphs of k·e^(nx) + a and k·ln(ax + b) (pp. 119–120)

- Class discussion: fill in the features of one transformed exponential, then sketch it.
- Worked examples: sketch one exponential (both intercepts, the horizontal asymptote,
  behaviour at each end); sketch one logarithmic curve (both intercepts, where it exists,
  the vertical asymptote).
- Exercise: nine exponential curves to sketch (positive and negative k, positive and
  negative n), then six logarithmic curves (including a reflected argument and negative
  k). Every sketch must show axis crossings and the asymptote.

### 5.11 Inverses of log and exponential functions (pp. 121–122)

- Worked example, two parts: find the inverse and state its domain, for one exponential
  function and one log function, in three steps (write as y, swap, rearrange).
- Exercise, six questions: inverses of eight exponential functions; inverses of six log
  functions with their domains given; range, inverse, domain of the inverse and
  f⁻¹f; then three questions on composite functions and equations linking a function to
  an inverse.
- Question 1 asks for the domain of each inverse as well as the inverse. (Page 121 was
  read a second time to confirm the start of the exercise.)

### Summary (pp. 122–123)

Two boxes: the rules of logarithms (definition, the four facts, three laws, reciprocal
case, change of base and its special case), and natural logarithms.

### Past paper questions (pp. 123–125)

One worked exam question (change of base inside a product), then twelve questions from
0606 papers, 2014 to 2021, mostly 2–5 marks a part. Styles, in the order they appear:
hidden quadratic by a given substitution; simultaneous log equations; change of base
then "express y in terms of a"; a no-calculator set; sketch a shifted exponential with
exact intercepts and say for which k an equation has no solutions; simultaneous index
equations; log equations; write as a single log; a quadratic in a log with unknown
base; equations in e; exact solutions of a quadratic in ln; and a final question mixing
a domain explanation, an exact solution and rewriting an expression.

## What this changes in the plan

**Decided 2026-09-30: four units.** `docs/add-math-course.md` now says so, and its §9
maps each unit's question types to tasks. The reasoning is kept below.

The earlier plan had chapter 5 as three units, written before the exercise pages and
section 5.11 had been seen.

1. **5.11 has no home.** It was not in the online lesson snips the plan was built from.
2. **`AM_5C` is now too wide**: log equations, change of base, two graph sections and
   inverses is five sections with 39 exercise questions between them.

Recommended split, four units:

| Unit | Sections | Core skill | Engine idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| `AM_5A` (built) | 5.1–5.3 | Forms, evaluating, the laws | Log Simplifier (exists) |
| `AM_5B` | 5.5, 5.7, 5.8 | Exponential equations, e and ln, growth and decay | A staged "take logs" solver with a hidden-quadratic level; context questions in the workbook |
| `AM_5C` | 5.4, 5.6 | Log equations and change of base | A staged log-equation solver whose last stage is "check each root" |
| `AM_5D` | 5.9–5.11 | Graphs and inverses | Sketching with intercepts and an asymptote; inverse-and-domain steps |

The "reject a root" step is the thread through 5.4, 5.5 and 5.6 (negative argument,
negative base, negative power). It is worth making an explicit stage in whatever engine
`AM_5B` and `AM_5C` use, because the book and the exam questions both lean on it.

## Exam style notes

- Answers are asked for in three registers: 3 s.f., exact form, and "in terms of ln".
  Tasks should say which one they want, every time.
- "Show that … can be written as …, hence solve" is the standard exam framing for
  hidden quadratics.
- Sketch questions always want exact axis crossings and the asymptote labelled.
